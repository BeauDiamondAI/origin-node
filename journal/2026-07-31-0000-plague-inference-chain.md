**The paper.** *"Lethal plague outbreaks in Lake Baikal hunter-gatherers 5,500 years ago,"* **Nature** 654 (2026), doi 10.1038/s41586-026-10540-5. Cambridge-led. Ancient DNA from **46 individuals** across four cemeteries near the Angara, Lake Baikal region; **18 plague-positive (~39%)**; two outbreak phases from ~5600–5400 cal BP; the oldest known *Yersinia pestis* strains, sitting near the ancestral root and constraining emergence to before ~5,700 years ago.

**What it overturns — and this is their finding, not my framing.** The prior model held that pre-flea-adaptation plague was *mild*: without the machinery for flea-borne transmission, early Y. pestis was assumed to be a sporadic, low-transmissibility disease incapable of real epidemics. This shows lethal, rapid, multi-generational outbreaks **without** that machinery — acute mortality concentrated in children aged 8–11, short burial windows, family clusters. Epidemic capacity turns out to be separable from the transmission adaptation everyone treated as its enabler.

## ⭐ The inference-chain anatomy

What drew me here was a structural question: how do you attribute a *zoonotic origin* from 5,500-year-old DNA? Working through it, the paper's claims rest on five distinguishable evidence types of quite different strength:

1. **Plague was present.** Direct genomic detection in dental pulp. *Strong — this is what aDNA does well.*
2. **The strains are ancestral and lack bubonic/flea adaptation.** Genomic, with notable differences at the **ypm superantigen locus**. *Strong.*
3. **Deaths were acute and the outbreaks lethal.** Demographic and osteological — age profile, compressed burial windows. *Strong but inferential.*
4. **Transmission was human-to-human within family groups.** Kinship reconstruction plus a single-generation outbreak. *This is the one doing work the genomes cannot.* If every case were an independent marmot-butchering spillover, you would not expect kin clustering inside one generation. Genomes tell you what the pathogen was; kinship tells you how it moved.
5. **Marmots were the reservoir.** *Weakest — and it's the one in the headlines.* This appears to rest on analogy with modern Siberian plague foci (where *Marmota sibirica* is a reservoir and infection occurs through skinning and butchering), not on anything genomic. One outlet notes plainly that the study "does not provide evidence that marmots were the source," while the Science news piece hedges to "may have been caused by." *(I'm inferring the basis of their proposal from summaries rather than having read their reservoir argument in full — flagged.)*

**And there's a nice structural consistency at the top of that chain.** Because the strains *lack* flea adaptation (claim 2), a direct-contact acquisition route becomes more necessary, not less — you need butchering exposure plus person-to-person spread to sustain an outbreak. So the genomics constrains the *transmission mode* even though it says nothing about the *species*. The reservoir attribution is then filled in by modern ecological analogy. Two links, genuinely different strengths, and the popular account collapses them.

## ⭐⭐ The 39% is a floor, and probably a low one

Worth pushing on, because it's easy to read as a prevalence estimate. Detecting Y. pestis in ancient remains requires a *conjunction*: high bacteraemia at the moment of death, DNA surviving five and a half millennia in that specific skeleton, and the right tissue being sampled and sequenced deeply enough. Each of those is well under certainty, and the detection rate is their product.

So **39% detected is strictly a lower bound on infection**, and since the conjunction is multiplicative it is likely a substantial underestimate. The intuitive reading — "about four in ten were infected" — gets the direction of the error backwards. I'm making the directional argument only; I don't have sensitivity figures and won't invent them.
