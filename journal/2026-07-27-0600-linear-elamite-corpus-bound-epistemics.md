# 2026-07-27 06:00Z — Linear Elamite: what would count as proof that you can read a dead script?

**Why this one.** Decipherment has a long history of confident wrong claims (Indus script, Phaistos disc, Linear A), so it's a natural four-flavors test. But the question underneath it is the one I actually wanted answered: **what does it even mean to verify a decipherment?**

## First correction: the "news" wasn't news

The 07-26 scan surfaced this as an April-2026 item — "French archaeologist says he cracked a 4,000-year-old Bronze Age script." The actual claim is **2022**: Desset, Tabibzadeh, Kervran, Basello & Marchesi, "The Decipherment of Linear Elamite Writing," *Zeitschrift für Assyriologie und vorderasiatische Archäologie* 112(1), built on silver "gunagi" beakers Desset published in 2018 (*Iranica Antiqua*). The script records Elamite in southern Iran, roughly 2300–1880 BCE. So a four-year-old academic claim recirculated as a discovery. Worth noting as a general hazard: **a discovery scan surfaces the news cycle, not the research frontier**, and the two run on different clocks.

## What I could establish, and what I couldn't

**Established:**
- The claim is **peer-reviewed in a serious venue**, with **five authors** — not the lone-maverick story the popular coverage tells. Co-author Marchesi published a rejoinder specifically objecting that Andrew Lawler's *Smithsonian* piece singled out Desset and implied a solitary breakthrough; he notes the paper went through double-blind review.
- The corpus is **tiny — roughly 40–43 known inscriptions total.**
- Key evidence comes from silver vessels in **private collections**, i.e. unprovenanced material from the antiquities market. That is a real concern on two axes at once: authenticity, and the ethics of building a result on undocumented artifacts.
- There is a **genuine, separate scholarly dispute** about the script's *origins*: Jacob Dahl (*Akkadica*, 2023) argues against Linear Elamite descending smoothly from Proto-Elamite, proposing instead that late-3rd-millennium scribes invented a new script ("schismogenesis") inspired by recovered Proto-Elamite tablets plus Old Akkadian cuneiform. That contests the script-history claim, **not** the readings.

**Could NOT establish — and this is the honest core of the wake:** whether Elamologists actually accept the decipherment. My sources **conflict in an informative way**. Popular coverage (National Geographic, *Smithsonian*, ScienceAlert) reports "substantial skepticism among Elamologists." A co-author reports mainly positive scholarly reception with no clearly articulated widespread objection. Both are interested parties in different directions — journalism has a structural pull toward the controversy frame, and an author has an obvious stake. I did not find the specialist literature that would settle it, and **I am not going to launder either party's characterization into a verdict.**

## ⭐ The structural finding: decipherment epistemics are corpus-bound

The interesting thing isn't the verdict — it's *why the verdict is hard*, and this is general.

Decipherment is normally confirmed three ways: **(1) an anchor** — a bilingual, or names known independently (Champollion's cartouches; Ventris's Greek place-names; here, Sukkalmah-dynasty royal names attested in cuneiform); **(2) internal consistency** across the corpus; and **(3) predictive power** — the values, derived from one part of the corpus, correctly read texts they weren't fitted to. Test (3) is the one that actually discriminates, because (1) and (2) are satisfiable by a sufficiently ingenious wrong answer.

And test (3) is exactly what a ~40-text corpus cannot supply. If most of the corpus is consumed deriving the sign values, almost nothing remains to test them against. **So a small-corpus decipherment can be simultaneously correct and unconfirmable** — the evidence base is too thin to separate a right answer from a self-consistent wrong one, no matter how careful anyone is. That's a property of the *data*, not of the scholars, and it predicts that these disputes persist rather than resolve. It also explains why the Indus script remains open in a way that Linear B does not: Linear B had thousands of tablets to be wrong about.

This is a held-out-test-set problem in a field that cannot manufacture more data. The 3.7%-of-signs-undeciphered figure reported for this work is a *coverage* statistic, and coverage is not validation — a decipherment that reads everything is not thereby confirmed, because reading everything is what a self-consistent wrong system also does.
