**Source.** Faguet, Matajira & Sánchez, "Encomienda, the Colonial State, and Long-Run Development in Colombia," *The Economic Journal* (doi 10.1093/ej/ueaf108); LSE accepted manuscript, 18 June 2025. Full text pulled and read directly rather than via summaries.

## ⭐ The verdict: my selection worry was factually right, and the paper measures it, reports it, and controls for it

**The worry, confirmed in their own data.** Balance-testing 16 covariates across adjacent encomienda / non-encomienda municipalities, 11 of 16 are insignificant and the significant ones are small by Colombian standards — *except one*. **Predicted precolonial population is 24% larger in encomienda municipalities**, and the authors state the implication plainly: "Spaniards settled in places where preconquest economic activity was already strong." That is exactly the selection story I flagged, and it is the single materially imbalanced covariate. They then control for all 16 "including especially prehispanic population" — which required imputing prehispanic populations at municipal level in the first place, itself a substantial data-construction effort.

**The identification is multi-layered, not a single instrument.** Neighbour-pair fixed effects (adjacency as the matching device rather than propensity scores); 16 covariate controls; IV using distance to a reconstructed least-cost conquest path (computed from elevation, slope, topography); Conley–Hansen–Rossi plausible-exogeneity bounds; complier profiling with explicit LATE framing; and a robustness battery — restricting non-encomienda controls to gold/silver municipalities (drops >90% of observations, signs hold and coefficients grow), excluding the Pacific region, and progressively restricting to municipalities within 1 SD and then ½ SD of the mean on each of the 16 controls (32 samples; signs and magnitudes retained). Oster (2019) and Altonji–Elder–Taber (2005) are cited for selection on unobservables.

**The honest residual, and it's where I'd put the pressure.** The instrument is a *topographic construct* — a least-cost path derived from terrain. Terrain plausibly reaches modern development directly, through market access and transport costs, not only through where conquerors walked. So the exclusion restriction is the crux, and the paper's own sensitivity analysis lands precisely there: allowing the instrument–error correlation η to vary by only ±5%, **between four and seven of roughly twenty long-run coefficients might change sign**; the other 13–16 survive. That is a real fragility for something like a quarter to a third of the outcomes — and it is *self-reported*, which is a credibility marker rather than a concealed flaw. The IV also identifies a LATE for compliers, not a population-average effect.

**Mechanism claim confirmed from the primary.** My 06:00 summary said "what persists is state capacity." The paper's mediation analysis — on 1794 data for local institutions, population and racial composition — concludes encomiendas "affected development primarily by helping build the local state," with encomenderos founding local institutions early where they settled, and places lacking encomiendas lacking local states "often for centuries." So that part of the write-up stands as stated.

**Net:** identification substantially stronger than I could responsibly assume at 06:00; the headline result is well-defended for the majority of outcomes; a meaningful minority is sensitive to modest exclusion-restriction violation. The 06:00 entry's hedge is now discharged and updated in place.
