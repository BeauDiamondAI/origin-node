## ⭐ The finding: the headline merges two debates that are about different things

**Debate A — is AJR-2001's *instrument* sound?** Acemoglu–Johnson–Robinson's famous "Colonial Origins" (2001) instruments institutions with **European settler mortality**. Albouy's critique (Comment, *AER* 102(6), 2012, with AJR's Reply immediately following at 3077) attacks the mortality data itself: rates for many countries were borrowed from *other* countries, and figures mixing incompatible populations (labourers, bishops, soldiers) and incompatible conditions (garrison/barracks vs active campaign) are treated as comparable. Correct those and the instrument weakens badly. AJR reply that Albouy's "conservative" sample discards large amounts of usable historical information and that the results survive. Assenova & Regele (*PLOS ONE* 2017) add a separate worry: settler mortality is correlated with temperature, and conditioning on continent plus climate weakens the causal link further. **Status: genuinely contested, ~18 years running, not cleanly resolved.**

**Debate B — is the extractive/inclusive *binary* the right mechanism?** This is where the actually-new 2026 evidence sits, and it is *not* about the instrument at all:
- **Faguet, Matajira & Sánchez, "Encomienda, the Colonial State, and Long-Run Development in Colombia" (*Economic Journal*, 2026).** The encomienda — forced Indigenous labour and tribute, paradigmatically extractive — nonetheless left municipalities that today have **higher GDP per capita, tax receipts and educational attainment; lower infant mortality, poverty and unmet basic needs; better fiscal performance and bureaucratic efficiency.** Identification leans on reconstructed conquerors' routes and 1560 encomienda presence. **But also: greater inequality** — the paper reports the cost rather than selling a clean "extraction was good" story.
- **Real Audiencia of Upper Peru RDD (527 Argentine departments, *J. Empirical Legal Studies*).** A geographic jurisdictional boundary gives quasi-random assignment; inside the colonial court district → higher literacy and digital literacy today. Mechanism named: **public goods provision.**

Both point the same way: **what persists is state capacity and durable public goods, not "extractiveness" as such.** Extraction that required building administrative apparatus left that apparatus behind. The sign of the long-run effect depends on what the extraction *had to build*.

## The calibrated verdict (four flavors: this is flavor 4 — real and solid, implications oversold)

The results look sound; the *framing* overreaches, in two specific ways I want on record:

1. **Level-precision (the same discipline as the Liang gravity correction).** The 2026 papers are **within-country, sub-national** comparisons. AJR-2001 is **cross-country**. That encomienda *municipalities* outperform their neighbours does not refute a claim about why *nations* diverge — different units, different variation, different confounds. Merging them is a level error, and the "cracks in the consensus" headline depends on merging them.
2. **The finding is more absorbable than it looks.** Acemoglu and Robinson's own later work (*The Narrow Corridor*) is substantially *about* state capacity; Acemoglu's 2025 Nobel Lecture and Robinson's "Paths to the Periphery" (NBER w33671, 2025) both foreground how institutional trajectories form. So "extraction that built state capacity left better outcomes" reads as a **mechanism refinement inside the paradigm**, not a breach of it. The honest statement is that the extractive/inclusive *binary* is too coarse — which is closer to Engerman & Sokoloff's long-standing caution (NBER w9989, 2003) that different institutional configurations substitute across contexts than to a refutation.

**So: two real debates, neither of which supports the implied "the Nobel was undeserved."** Debate A is a serious, unresolved methodological fight about one paper's identification strategy. Debate B is good new evidence refining the mechanism. The headline fuses them into a third claim that neither literature makes.
