## The setup, and the inversion

The standard **return-on-investment** framework: symbiotic nitrogen fixation is carbon-expensive — **16 g C per 1 g N fixed** (Gutschick 1981; Fisher et al. 2010), plus nodule biomass running **4.1–13.6% of belowground biomass** (Menge et al. 2024). Expensive things get bought only when needed, so SNF should be downregulated when soil N is abundant.

The motivating paradox: it often isn't. High SNF rates show up *under high N availability*, and specifically under drought, high light, and elevated CO₂.

The proposed resolution rests on one physiological fact doing all the work: **moderate drought inhibits leaf growth but maintains photosynthesis.** That's source–sink decoupling — growth is more sensitive to water potential than carbon fixation is — so carbon accumulates. And the same holds for the other paradoxical conditions: high light and elevated CO₂ both raise supply without raising sink capacity.

**So under sink-limitation, exporting carbon to symbionts isn't paying a price. It's disposal, with nitrogen as a byproduct.** The 16:1 ratio doesn't change; what changes is its sign. Under source-limitation it's a cost to be justified. Under sink-limitation it's *sink capacity* — a use for carbon the plant has no better use for, and which can otherwise cause feedback inhibition of photosynthesis as non-structural carbohydrates pile up.

That's genuinely elegant, and parsimonious in the way the authors claim: one mechanism accounts for the whole cluster of paradoxical observations, because drought, high light and elevated CO₂ are all carbon-surplus conditions.

**The generalization is mine, and I'll mark it as such:** an optimality framework's *cost* term silently presupposes which resource is binding. The ROI model assumes carbon is scarce. Whether carbon is scarce is *conditional* — and when the binding constraint moves, the model's predictions don't merely weaken, they **invert**. The authors make the domain-specific version of this point; the general form is my restatement, not their claim.

**Honest status.** This is a parsimony argument, not evidence. The authors are clear about it, recommending isotopic tracing of C and N and monitoring of non-structural carbohydrate pools under C-surplus conditions. A hypothesis that explains a paradox cheaply is a good hypothesis and not yet a finding — and the failure mode here is obvious and worth naming: surplus-C is flexible enough to accommodate almost any observation after the fact, which is exactly why the proposed NSC-tracking test matters.
