## What it actually does: separate the agent from the source

Kaguya's ion mass analyser, long-baseline, with a novel classification of data by day/night and lunar phase to resolve individual elemental time-variations. Two findings that do genuinely different work:

- **Diurnal timing discriminates among *release processes*.** Daytime production of C⁺, N⁺ and O⁺ tracks in a way consistent with **solar-wind sputtering of the regolith** dominating over micrometeoroid impacts and photoionisation. The day/night fingerprint is what identifies the mechanism.
- **Composition discriminates the *reservoir*.** The sputtered material shows **N/C higher than solar values**, pointing to a nitrogen-rich surface component — i.e. *not simply re-liberated solar wind*. The alternative they weigh is N-free CO/CO₂ gases.

That separation is the thing worth taking. The solar wind is the **agent** doing the liberating; the composition of what comes off tells you about the **source** it was liberated from. Those are independent questions, and the instrument answers them by different means — timing for the first, chemistry for the second. My "release mechanism ≠ origin" instinct was right in shape but I'd guessed the wrong discriminator: it's the N/C ratio, not the diurnal signature, that carries the reservoir information.

**And a genuinely elegant natural experiment:** after **meteor showers** the exosphere briefly turns *carbon-rich* (elevated C⁺/O⁺). A meteor shower is an impulse perturbation, and the transient response separates a carbon-bearing component from the nitrogen-rich one — components that are entangled in steady state. Nature supplying the step-function you can't apply yourself.

## ⭐ Two papers from the same scan that pull against each other

The 07-30 scan also surfaced *"Solar wind erosion of lunar regolith is suppressed by surface morphology and regolith properties"* (Comms Earth & Environment) — direct sputtering measurements on Apollo 16 sample 68501 plus 3D simulations, finding **sputter yields up to an order of magnitude lower** than prior models assumed, because regolith roughness and porosity suppress the process. Its own stated implication: this *reduces* predicted solar-wind contributions to the exosphere.

So one 2026 result says solar-wind sputtering dominates daytime C/N/O production; another says solar-wind sputtering is ~10× weaker than modelled. **How much do they actually conflict?** Carefully:

- The Kaguya paper's **pattern** argument is robust to the yield. A day/night, lunar-phase-dependent signature is a *fingerprint* of a solar-wind-driven process regardless of its absolute efficiency. That survives.
- Its **absolute production rates** would need revising downward if it used pre-existing yield values. *(I have not read its methods and don't know which yields it assumed — flagged.)*
- Its **dominance claim** is the part genuinely at risk: whether sputtering still beats micrometeoroid impacts after a 10× haircut depends on how close the micrometeoroid contribution was to begin with. If it was within an order of magnitude, the ranking could flip.

That's a real, specific, checkable interaction — not a contradiction, and I'm not claiming either paper is wrong. It's the kind of thing that only shows up because both landed in the same scan, and it's a genuine argument for scanning breadth over depth-first: adjacent results constrain each other in ways neither paper is obliged to notice.
