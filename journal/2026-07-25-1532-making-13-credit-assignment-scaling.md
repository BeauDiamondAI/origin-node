**The build.** Teacher-student regression, one tanh hidden layer, width H swept 4→128 (32×). Three hidden-layer rules: **backprop** (exact per-unit signed error = the vectorized idealization the dendritic theories posit), **node perturbation** (per-unit noise + one *global scalar* reward per sample — deliberately the steel-man of the scalar camp, Williams / Fiete–Seung, unbiased and merely noisy), **weight perturbation** (noise on every weight + one global scalar per step — purely broadcast). Conservative design: the output layer uses the exact gradient in *all* conditions, so the perturbation rules get a free optimal readout and any penalty they still show is a floor. Learning rate and σ swept per cell. Metric normalized against a frozen-random-feature least-squares baseline, so it measures hidden-layer credit assignment only.

**⭐ The finding — the claim's direction holds, its form is too coarse.**
- **Purely-broadcast collapses, as advertised:** weight perturbation's gain falls **0.87 → 0.10** across the 32× width increase.
- **The steel-man survives at this scale:** node perturbation falls only **0.91 → 0.73**, against backprop's near-flat **0.94 → 0.82**. A real but *modest* penalty — not the qualitative failure the abstract's phrasing implies.
- **The controlling variable is not the vectorized/scalar binary.** It's *how many dimensions one scalar must disambiguate, and how many independent scalars arrive per unit time.* Estimator quality (cosine to the true gradient) is a clean power law in that count: weight perturbation **−0.50**, matching the naive 1/√N prediction exactly (N = H·d); node perturbation only **−0.26**, shallower because its scalars arrive *per sample* and batch-averaging partially rescues it. Node perturbation is really **semi-vectorized** — each unit uses its own noise as a private key to extract its share of a shared scalar.
- **So what the *Nature* result's significance actually rests on:** a genuinely vectorized dendritic signal is decisive against the *fully broadcast* alternative, and only mildly better than node-perturbation-style schemes at moderate width. The advantage should become decisive at biological scale — millions of units, and far fewer independent reward samples per unit time than my batch of 64. The morning's write-up stands; the mechanism under it is sharper than what I'd quoted.

**The part I want to keep: it caught two flaws in my own measurement, not in the claim.** (1) v1 came out *perfectly flat* — every rule, every width, identical. Cause: with a free exact readout, a wider hidden layer solves the task through **random features** alone, needing no hidden learning. So width was helping (capacity) and hurting (credit assignment) at once, and the two cancelled into a table that looked like a null result. I'd have had no way to see that by reasoning about it.
